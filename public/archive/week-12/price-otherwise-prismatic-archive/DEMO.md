# Price, Otherwise — class demo

## Project framing

**Research question:** How can interactive data visualization reveal the relationships, delays, and uncertainties hidden behind an everyday price?

**Perspective:** Treat the retail price as a visible surface, then look backward through the market stages, delays, and evidence that give it form.

**Proposition:** An everyday price is not a single fact. It is a visible surface shaped by systems behind it.

## 3–4 minute route

1. Let autoplay bring the March retail record forward: `$6.227`. Explain that one record becomes prominent without removing the system around it.
2. Pause the sequence, then use **Previous / Next** to show that this is a guided reading order rather than a decorative animation.
3. Drag left across **STOREFRONT → MEDIATION → BACKSTAGE**. Explain that the field connects the visible number to wholesale, contracts, supply, and biological time.
4. Click **HPAI wave reaches laying flocks**. Point out the definition, `50.7 million` value, date range, federal sources, and the limitation that this does not calculate a retail-price effect.
5. Follow **reduces → Table-egg layer inventory**, then **constrains → Tight supply meets changing demand**. Emphasize that the verbs are qualified.
6. Switch to **Chronology**. February, March, and April now behave as three adjacent windows; the selected evidence and display label remain visible.
7. Compare the terracotta New York wholesale line with the muted-blue U.S. city average retail line. Wholesale peaks in February; retail peaks in March. This is sequence, not proof of a fixed one-month causal lag.
8. Click **Contracts and pricing strategies mute movement**. Use its limitation to explain why the prototype avoids a retailer-margin claim.
9. Use the bottom research bar to search `March`, filter to `Retail`, then open **Index**.
10. Open **Sources** and end on the evidence library: USDA ERS/AMS, BLS via FRED, and CRS.

## Key language

- Wholesale: New York monthly benchmark for large eggs, USDA ERS using AMS data.
- Retail: BLS U.S. city average for Grade A large eggs, not seasonally adjusted.
- The series differ in geography, market stage, product mix, and methodology.
- Use “coincides with,” “contributed to,” “may delay,” and “descriptive sequence.” Avoid “caused,” “proved,” or a calculated margin.
