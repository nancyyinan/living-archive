# Three-Week Thesis Research Schedule

**November 16–December 6, 2026**

**From Abstract Ideas to a Tangible Research Subject**

**Focus:** research, evidence collection, scope definition, and early visualization experiments

## Purpose

The goal of these three weeks is not to finish a mature data-visualization prototype. It is to identify a tangible subject that I genuinely care about, define the central question of the thesis, and test its research potential with real evidence.

The process moves through three stages:

1. **Exploration:** identify and compare concrete research subjects.
2. **Investigation:** study one subject like a journalist and collect multiple forms of evidence.
3. **Thesis direction:** connect the evidence, test early visualization ideas, and formulate a focused thesis question.

---

## Week 1 — Explore & Narrow

**November 16–22, 2026**

**Weekly goal:** Move from broad interests—such as AI, collective memory, and historical change—toward a small number of concrete, researchable subjects.

### Monday, November 16 — Reframe the research interest

- Review the November 13 meeting notes and previous thesis work.
- Separate the three layers of the project: research interest, research subject, and possible design outcome.
- Write a short statement explaining what I am trying to understand before considering how to visualize it.

**Daily output:** A working research-interest statement and a list of selection criteria for a tangible subject.

### Tuesday, November 17 — Generate tangible subjects

- List 5–7 possible objects, places, infrastructures, records, or bounded events.
- Include at least one subject connected to my own experience or long-term curiosity.
- Reduce the list to three candidates that are specific enough to investigate.

**Daily output:** Three candidate subjects with a one-sentence reason for choosing each one.

### Wednesday, November 18 — Run an evidence test

For each candidate, try to locate:

- One concrete object or place.
- One event with a clear date and location.
- One quantitative record.
- One human voice or public account.
- One contradiction between different perspectives.

**Daily output:** A comparable five-part evidence sample for each candidate.

### Thursday, November 19 — Compare the candidates

- Compare the three subjects by personal connection, relevance, available evidence, accessibility, manageable scope, and potential for relational storytelling.
- Note where a subject is still too broad or depends on inaccessible information.
- Identify what each subject could reveal that a conventional timeline would not.

**Daily output:** A three-subject comparison matrix.

### Friday, November 20 — Select the primary subject

- Choose one primary research subject and one backup.
- Define the subject as precisely as possible: what it is, where it is, who is involved, and which time span matters.
- Draft 2–3 provisional research questions without assuming a conclusion.

**Daily output:** A selected primary subject, a defined scope, and a provisional question.

### Saturday, November 21 — Test the subject’s research potential

- Conduct a focused source search on the selected subject.
- Identify possible public records, datasets, reporting, community accounts, images, maps, and archival materials.
- Record access limitations and any ethical or factual risks.

**Daily output:** A preliminary source map and feasibility notes.

### Sunday, November 22 — Synthesize Week 1

- Write a one-page research direction statement.
- Explain why this subject matters to me, what larger system it connects to, and what I still need to learn.
- Review the scope and revise the provisional question.

**Week 1 deliverables:**

- One-page research direction statement.
- Comparison of three tangible subjects.
- One selected primary research subject.
- Provisional research question and preliminary source map.

## Week 2 — Investigate & Collect

**November 23–29, 2026**

**Weekly goal:** Investigate the selected subject like a journalist, gathering different forms of evidence rather than immediately designing the interface.

> November 26 is Thanksgiving in the United States, so the second half of this week remains intentionally flexible.

### Monday, November 23 — Build the investigation structure

- Turn the provisional question into 4–6 research subquestions.
- Create evidence categories: quantitative, qualitative, historical, spatial, institutional, and personal.
- Establish a source log that records title, author or organization, date, link or location, evidence type, reliability, and relevance.

**Daily output:** Research subquestions, evidence categories, and a source-log template.

### Tuesday, November 24 — Collect quantitative and spatial evidence

- Gather available datasets, measurements, maps, permits, budgets, utility records, or other structured evidence relevant to the subject.
- Record units, time ranges, geographic boundaries, missing values, and collection methods.
- Avoid treating correlation as causation.

**Daily output:** A documented quantitative and spatial evidence set.

### Wednesday, November 25 — Collect historical and institutional evidence

- Build a factual chronology of major decisions and events.
- Collect policy documents, planning records, company or institutional statements, news reports, and archival materials.
- Note which actors created each record and what perspective may be absent.

**Daily output:** A sourced working chronology and institutional evidence folder.

### Thursday, November 26 — Thanksgiving / light review

- Keep the day flexible; no major research milestone is required.
- If time allows, clean filenames, update citations, and note missing sources.

**Daily output:** Optional archive cleanup and a short list of gaps.

### Friday, November 27 — Collect human voices

- Gather public testimony, interviews, community-meeting records, first-person accounts, or other relevant perspectives.
- Distinguish direct testimony from paraphrase and secondary reporting.
- Identify whose voices are represented and whose are still missing.

**Daily output:** A documented qualitative evidence set and a representation-gap note.

### Saturday, November 28 — Map stakeholders and contradictions

- Identify the people, communities, institutions, companies, agencies, and resources connected to the subject.
- Record what each stakeholder claims, wants, controls, gains, risks, or contests.
- Identify at least 2–3 meaningful relationships or contradictions supported by evidence.

**Daily output:** Stakeholder overview and 2–3 evidence-based relationships.

### Sunday, November 29 — Audit and organize the archive

- Organize all evidence by type, source, date, place, and stakeholder.
- Label each item as verified fact, stakeholder claim, official response, or my own inference.
- Write a list of unknowns, contradictions, missing voices, and next research steps.

**Week 2 deliverables:**

- Organized, source-documented evidence archive.
- Stakeholder overview.
- Two or three meaningful, evidence-based relationships.
- List of what remains unknown.

## Week 3 — Connect & Define

**November 30–December 6, 2026**

**Weekly goal:** Use the collected research to establish the thesis proposition and explore how relational visualization could reveal something that a conventional timeline cannot.

### Monday, November 30 — Identify patterns

- Review the archive without designing the interface yet.
- Mark recurring actors, places, resources, decisions, conflicts, and changes over time.
- Write 5–7 observations that are directly supported by evidence.

**Daily output:** Evidence-backed observation list.

### Tuesday, December 1 — Build a relational map

- Create a map connecting people, institutions, places, records, resources, and events.
- Annotate every important connection with its supporting source.
- Distinguish documented relationships from questions or hypotheses.

**Daily output:** First evidence-based relational map.

### Wednesday, December 2 — Define the thesis proposition

- Review what becomes visible when different evidence types are connected.
- Refine the research question so it is specific, open-ended, and answerable through the selected subject.
- Draft a short thesis proposition explaining the subject, the question, the evidence, and why relational visualization is appropriate.

**Daily output:** Refined thesis question and draft thesis proposition.

### Thursday, December 3 — Develop visualization test 1

- Choose one meaningful relationship from the map.
- Create a low-fidelity experiment that lets a viewer move among multiple evidence types or perspectives.
- Test what the structure reveals beyond a linear timeline.

**Daily output:** Exploratory visualization test 1 with notes.

### Friday, December 4 — Develop visualization test 2

- Explore a contrasting entry point or interaction structure using the same evidence.
- Compare how starting from a person, place, resource, event, or document changes the viewer’s understanding.
- Record what is clarified, obscured, or overstated in each test.

**Daily output:** Exploratory visualization test 2 and a comparison of the two approaches.

### Saturday, December 5 — Prepare critique materials

- Select the clearest evidence, relationships, and visual experiments.
- Prepare a short presentation that explains the shift from an abstract visualization system to a specific investigation.
- Include the research question, subject, methodology, evidence, relational map, experiments, limitations, and next steps.

**Daily output:** Draft critique presentation.

### Sunday, December 6 — Final review and package

- Check every major claim against its source.
- Make sure facts, stakeholder claims, responses, and inferences remain visibly distinct.
- Revise the thesis question, relational map, and presentation based on the final review.
- Package all materials for critique and continued thesis development.

**Week 3 deliverables:**

- Refined thesis question and thesis proposition.
- Evidence-based relational map.
- One or two exploratory visualization tests.
- Short presentation for critique.

## Final Research Package — December 6

By the end of the three weeks, the package should contain:

1. One-page research direction statement.
2. Three-subject comparison and selection rationale.
3. Clearly bounded primary research subject.
4. Refined thesis question and short thesis proposition.
5. Source-documented evidence archive containing quantitative, qualitative, historical, spatial, institutional, and personal material where available.
6. Source log and working chronology.
7. Stakeholder overview and representation-gap note.
8. Two or three evidence-based relationships or contradictions.
9. List of unknowns, limitations, and next research steps.
10. Evidence-based relational map.
11. One or two exploratory visualization tests.
12. Short critique presentation.

## Methodology

### Work like a journalist before working like a visualization designer

Begin with a real subject and a question, not with a preferred interface. Investigate the subject deeply enough to understand why the records should be connected.

### Use a tangible entry point to approach a larger system

The subject may be one physical place, object, record, infrastructure, or bounded event. Its value is that it provides a manageable entry point into broader social, technological, environmental, or historical relationships.

### Gather multiple forms of evidence

Combine quantitative records with qualitative accounts, historical documents, spatial evidence, institutional records, and personal perspectives. Preserve the source and context of every item.

### Separate evidence from interpretation

Clearly distinguish:

- Verified facts.
- Stakeholder claims or personal testimony.
- Institutional or company responses.
- Research observations and inferences.

Do not assume causation simply because two changes occur at the same time.

### Let relationships emerge before choosing the form

First ask what becomes visible when the records connect. Only then decide how visualization and interaction can help others explore those relationships.

### Preserve complexity without losing focus

The purpose is not to force the subject into a simple good-versus-bad conclusion. It is to present multiple perspectives while maintaining a clear, bounded research question.

## Workflow Shift

Instead of:

> Collect data → Design visualization → Find relationships → Explain the project

Use:

> Tangible subject → Evidence and investigation → Relationships and observations → Visualization and interaction

The central question throughout the process is:

> What am I trying to understand, and what becomes visible when these records are connected?
